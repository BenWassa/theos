Christian practice is built to be done with the body, in company, over time, not merely believed in private.

**The sacraments** are its physical core: acts in which ordinary matter is treated as a carrier of grace. Water poured at baptism. Bread and wine shared at the Eucharist, understood by many as the real presence of Christ and by others as a memorial, but by nearly all as the beating centre of worship. The count and meaning vary by church, but the instinct is shared, that the invisible is met through the tangible.

**Time itself is shaped.** The liturgical year walks the community through the whole story annually: the waiting of Advent, the birth at Christmas, the fasting and self-examination of Lent, the shock of Good Friday, the release of Easter. To live inside it is to rehearse dying and rising every single year until it sinks below the level of argument.

**Prayer runs underneath all of it**, spoken, sung, silent; alone at a bedside and together in a full nave. Practice, here, is less a set of rules than a repeated turning of the whole self toward God until the turning becomes a shape the person is bent into.

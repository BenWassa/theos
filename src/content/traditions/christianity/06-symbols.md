The central symbol is an instrument of torture. A cross — the Roman machinery of a slow public death — is worn at the throat, raised over altars, tattooed onto skin. Christianity's boldest move is to take the worst thing the empire could do to a body and make it the sign of hope. Every crucifix insists that suffering is not the last word about suffering.

Then there is **light**. Cathedrals are engineered to catch it: stained glass shatters the sun into colour and lays it across stone floors, so that the room itself preaches without saying anything. Candles are lit for the dead and the desperate; a single flame in a dark side-chapel carries a weight out of all proportion to its size.

The **table** gathers the rest — bread torn, wine poured, a shared meal at the centre of the whole thing. Add the sensory grammar of incense and chant and bells, and the effect is deliberate: to reach the body before it reaches the mind, so that belief arrives less as an argument than as a room you have already been standing in.

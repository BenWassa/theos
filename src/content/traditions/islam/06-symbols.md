Islam's sensory world is shaped by a refusal. Wary that any image of God, or of the prophets, might slide into idolatry, much of the tradition turned away from figurative religious art and poured that same creative force into two channels instead: the **word** and the **pattern**.

So its holiest art is writing. Arabic calligraphy raises the letters of the Qur'an into architecture, verses that stretch across a dome, curl around an archway, dissolve into knotwork so intricate the meaning and the ornament become the same gesture. And where other traditions paint faces, Islam builds **geometry**: interlacing stars and tessellations that repeat without end, a visual argument for a unity that has no centre and no edge because it is everywhere at once.

The defining sense, though, is **sound**. Five times a day the *adhan* rises over a city, a human voice, unaccompanied, stretching the name of God across the rooftops. It is not music exactly and not speech exactly. It is a reminder made audible, and for those raised inside it, its absence in a foreign city can feel like a light that has been switched off.

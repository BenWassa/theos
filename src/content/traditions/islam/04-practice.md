Islam organises the whole of a life around **five recurring acts**, and their genius is repetition. Faith is not asked to sustain itself on feeling; it is carried by schedule.

**Salah** — five prayers a day — cuts time into liturgical segments so that no stretch of ordinary life runs longer than a few hours without a return. **Zakat** takes a fixed share of one's wealth and moves it, annually, toward those with less, treating generosity as a due rather than a mood. **Sawm**, the month-long fast of Ramadan, empties the daylight of food and drink so that hunger becomes a teacher — of restraint, of gratitude, of solidarity with those who do not choose their hunger; and the shared meal at sundown turns a whole community's clock into one clock.

**Hajj**, the pilgrimage to Mecca undertaken once in a lifetime by those able, dissolves rank entirely: kings and labourers circle the same stone house in the same two sheets of unstitched white cloth, indistinguishable.

The pattern beneath all five is the same as the tradition's name — submission enacted on a timetable, until devotion is less a decision one makes each day than a groove one's days already run in.

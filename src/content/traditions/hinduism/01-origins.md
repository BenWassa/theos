Hinduism does not begin with a single creation so much as with a question about creation, and, remarkably, an ancient willingness to leave it open. *Who really knows?* asks one of its oldest hymns of the world's origin. *Perhaps it formed itself, or perhaps it did not, the one who looks down from the highest heaven, only he knows, or perhaps even he does not know.* Few scriptures anywhere open the door and then admit they cannot see all the way through it.

What emerges across the tradition is not a line but a **wheel**. Time does not run once from a beginning to an end; it turns through immense cycles, *yugas*, of creation, flourishing, decline, and dissolution, and then begins again, has begun again already, countless times. The universe breathes out and breathes in.

Beneath the dizzying surface, the many gods, the many worlds, much of the tradition points to a single reality without form or limit: **Brahman**. And its most vertiginous claim is that the innermost self of each person, the **Atman**, is not merely close to that ultimate reality but identical with it. *Tat tvam asi*, *that thou art.* The separation you feel, the sense of being a small self looking out at a vast and foreign cosmos, is named **Maya**: not a lie exactly, but a veil.
